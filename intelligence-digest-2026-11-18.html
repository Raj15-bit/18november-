The big picture: the AI trade cracked, and India caught the money that fell out

How the Nvidia crisis, rising US rates and the Gulf war fit together, and why India moved the other way.

In short
  • Big global funds are cutting their bets on AI shares faster than at any time since 2006.
  • India is where a lot of that money went: foreign funds bought a record $10bn of Indian shares in November so far.
  • But prices inside India are rising fast, and small company shares are still falling, so the gains are narrow.

3 big shocks hit at the same time. The founder of Nvidia, the chip company at the centre of the AI boom, quit over his private life and now wants to come back. The US central bank is raising interest rates again. And the war around Iran moved from ships to pipelines, airbases and Kuwait. The map below puts each force in one tile.

Money is leaving the AI trade, and part of it lands in India

Fund managers in the monthly BofA survey cut their holdings of technology shares by the most in one month since June 2006. A record share of them now say companies are spending too much on AI, and about half expect one of the big cloud companies to cut spending within 6 months.

  • Asia's view of India — Asian fund managers moved to overweight India for the first time in 11 months. India now ranks 4th in their list, behind Japan, Taiwan and Korea.
  • The see-saw — since 13 October the Nasdaq fell on 11 days; on 7 of them the Sensex rose the next day. On 6 November the Nasdaq lost 5.5% and the Sensex gained more than 2%.
  • Record month — foreign funds bought $10.07bn of Indian shares in November up to 17 November, beating the old record of $8.42bn from December 2020.
  • Still a bad year — even after that, foreign funds have taken out $21.3bn in 2026, the worst year since 2002.

One view is that India has become the "anti-AI trade": when money runs from crowded chip shares in Korea and Taiwan, it runs here, and the rupee firms while the dollar rises. The opposite view is that this money can leave as fast as it came: foreign traders still hold 2 bets on a fall for every 1 bet on a rise in index futures, and the next Budget's tax on foreign gains could decide whether they stay.

Why the 3 shocks are really 1 story

The AI build-out needs huge loans. Nvidia itself stands behind large loan guarantees for data centres, and banks expect hundreds of billions more in AI project loans next year. That borrowing pushes up US interest rates, and the war keeps oil-driven inflation alive, so the Fed hikes. Dearer money then makes the AI spending look riskier.

  • Nvidia — founder gone, an SEC probe, and lawmakers asking about chips that may have reached China (section 02).
  • The Fed — 1 governor wants a 0.50% rise at once; another says several hikes are needed by spring (section 03).
  • The war — tankers hit near Hormuz, a Saudi pipeline struck again, drones over Kuwait and Bahrain (section 04).
  • India — record foreign buying, the strongest company profit growth in 16 quarters, and a firmer rupee (sections 05 and 08).

Reading between the lines, India is not cut off from these shocks; it is paid by them for now. If AI spending is cut, chip-heavy markets lose and India gains. But if the war pushes oil higher again, India pays more than most, because it imports most of its crude.

Inside India, 2 speeds

The big names are doing well: banks and Reliance lead, and the Nifty is back above where it stood before the war began in February. But on one day most sector indices closed red after a green open, and dealers say small companies are hard to sell at all.

So the rally is a big-company rally. Foreign funds buy what they can buy in size, while local funds sell to them. Section 05 sets out who bought what.

02

Nvidia: record sales, no chief executive, and a growing pile of promises

The chip maker beat every forecast, but the fight over its founder and its role as lender to the AI industry now matter more than the sales.

In short
  • Nvidia's sales doubled in a year and beat forecasts, yet its founder Jensen Huang is gone and its board says he will not return.
  • Nvidia has promised or guaranteed hundreds of billions of dollars to suppliers, data centres and young cloud firms.
  • That makes it the bank of the AI boom, and the bank is now being run by an interim boss under several probes.

What happened at the top

Jensen Huang resigned on 12 November after affairs came to light, one of them with a woman suspected of spying for China. On 17 November he said he would be "honoured to return" if the board wanted. Board chair Harvey Jones said the board does not intend to bring him back, and finance chief Colette Kress runs the company for now.

  • Probes — the US securities regulator is checking whether Nvidia misled investors; the Justice Department is running a criminal case on Megaspeed, the Singapore firm linked to the alleged spy. Huang faces a House hearing on 1 December.
  • Pressure to return — a senior director's letter warned the board of losing "irreplaceable" talent; some directors still push for Huang, and filings show he can name 2 directors.
  • New guard rail — the board set up a national security and export control committee led by director Suzanne Nora Johnson.
  • The search — a search firm is looking inside and outside; names reported include Arm's Rene Haas and Marvell's Matt Murphy.

One view is that the company runs on its products, not one man, and that Huang's return was ruled out. The opposite view is that only a proxy fight can bring him back, and that staff, partners and many investors want exactly that. Betting markets put his return at about 13% to 15%.

The price of money: the Fed turns to hikes, and India's prices run hot

US rates, India's inflation, the rupee and the sharp fall in gold and silver.

In short
  • US central bankers now argue about how fast to raise rates, not when to cut them.
  • India's shop prices broke the RBI's upper limit and factory-gate prices rose more than 12%.
  • Gold has fallen about 30% from its peak, which hurts gold lenders and jewellers.

The Fed is hiking, and some want to go faster

The Fed raised rates in September for the first time since 2023, and markets now see a December rise as very likely. Governor Christopher Waller said the Fed should move by 0.50% now, because most service prices are rising faster than 3%, and the cause is not oil.

  • Musalem — the St Louis Fed head says several hikes are likely needed by spring.
  • Import prices — US import prices rose 1.1% in October, about double the forecast, and are up 8.1% in a year against 3.7% for shop prices.
  • US shoppers — retail sales rose 0.2%; the core part rose 0.6%, the strongest 3-month pace in over 4 years.
  • Druckenmiller — the investor says "the Fed is not cutting, they're hiking", is short copper and sits mostly in cash.

One view is that only a falling stock market will stop the hikes, which some call the "Warsh put", after Fed chair Kevin Warsh. The opposite view is that the Fed is now modelling a crash on purpose and will keep going until people stop expecting 3% inflation. Waller himself said he sees nothing like a bubble in AI prices.

Shop prices rose 6.09% in October, up from 5.54%, with food the main push. Wholesale prices, which track what factories and traders pay, rose much faster, the most in the new series. The chart shows where it comes from.

Companies are either eating the cost, which squeezes their profit, or they will pass it on, which pushes shop prices up later. That is why a December rate rise by the RBI is widely expected, even with the rupee firmer.

The rupee firmed while the dollar rose

The rupee closed at 93.92 per dollar on 16 November, its strongest in about 7 months. Over 3 months the dollar index rose, yet the rupee gained against the dollar, helped by foreign buying and RBI steps from mid-October.

  • Forward market — prices for end-December point to about 94.3, firmer than the economists' middle forecast of about 95.5.
  • Forecasts — Morgan Stanley's base case is 96 by end-2027; another report sees 100 by June 2027 if AI money reverses.
  • Reserves — $719.5bn in the week to 6 November, up $2.49bn but well below September's $785.7bn record.

Gold and silver fall hard

Gold fell to about $3,775 an ounce on 18 November, close to its 52-week low and about 30% below its February peak. Silver is having its worst year in decades.

  • Indian gold futures — near ₹1,38,400, against a peak near ₹1,70,000.
  • The peak — gold traded above $5,000 an ounce in February.

One view is that central banks will keep buying gold while everyone else sells, which is Goldman's 2027 playbook. The opposite view is that a rising dollar and the highest US yields this century leave little room for gold to recover soon.

Cracks in other bond markets

  • Japan — insurers carry about $188bn of paper losses on long Japanese government bonds, 10 times more than 3 years ago; 18 of 97 long bonds issued since 2016 have lost more than half their value.
  • Yuan bonds — Western banks have sold about $20bn of yuan bonds this year, about 3 times 2025, because China borrows at about 1.6% against 4.9% in the US.
  • Silver supply — silver is in its 6th year of shortfall and mine output peaked in 2016, even as prices fall.
  • US margin debt — money borrowed to buy US shares is $1.45T, near its record.

War, oil and ships: the fight moves from tankers to pipelines and bases

The US-Iran war near Hormuz, the attacks on Saudi Arabia's bypass pipeline, the Red Sea, and what it does to fuel, gas and fertiliser.

In short
  • Attacks spread from tankers to a Saudi oil pipeline, US bases and Kuwait, yet crude oil got slightly cheaper.
  • The pain sits in jet fuel, gas in Europe and sulfur for fertiliser, not in the crude price.
  • An Indian crew member is missing after an LPG tanker was hit near Hormuz.

What happened on the ground

Iran's leaders threatened civilian sites near US and Israeli bases, and there is no sign of a deal. One Iranian news agency said Iran is "seriously pursuing" a way out, while an Arab channel reported no progress at all.

The pipeline that lets Saudi oil avoid Hormuz is under fire

Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline carries crude 1,200 km from Abqaiq to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, so it is the main way around Hormuz. By Houthi claims, 7 of its pumping stations were hit in November, and Saudi Arabia has confirmed only one of those strikes.

  • Where they hit — the strikes sit on the high stations that push oil up into the Hijaz mountains, which are hardest to replace.
  • Repair time — estimates run from 1 to 3 months per station.
  • The exit — the Bab el-Mandeb route carries about 13% of world trade, about 30% of container traffic and about 7 million barrels of oil a day.
  • Help at sea — Saudi Arabia asked the EU for naval escorts; a French warship is already escorting ships.

Reading between the lines, attacking the bypass is aimed at the one thing that kept oil prices calm: Saudi barrels reaching buyers without crossing Hormuz. If both Hormuz and the Red Sea exit are squeezed together, the cheap crude price will not last.

Iran, Iraq and the big powers

  • Inside Iran — bread is rationed in Khuzestan and Zanjan, and factories lack gas.
  • Iraq — for the first time in more than 20 years its army is moving against an Iran-aligned militia. Iraqi crude is replacing Iranian barrels for Chinese refiners as the US blockade bites.
  • Russia and Ukraine — the Crimean bridge was hit 2 nights in a row and part of it collapsed; about 800 drones flew towards Moscow.
  • Shenzhen — Trump, Xi and Putin met and shook hands, the first meeting of the 3 sitting leaders together.

One view is that strikes peak just before big summits, so a pause on attacks against energy sites could follow Shenzhen. The other view is that the US is now fighting on an Iraqi front too, and that Iran's security chief openly says America will leave the Gulf, so neither side sees a reason to stop.

SEBI and money borrowed to buy shares

Margin trading lets investors buy shares with money lent by the broker. That book has reached about ₹1.53 lakh crore, up 29% in a year, and most of it sits in small and mid-sized companies. Reuters reported SEBI may cap new margin loans once they reach a set share of a company's free float (the shares not held by promoters).

  • SEBI's answer — the chair said no decision has been taken on changing the margin rules.
  • Brokers' view — Jefferies sees little impact: at worst about 4% of Groww's profit, and 2-3% of CDSL's revenue. Macquarie still expects the book to grow about 25% a year.
  • Small shares — more than 220 shares already have margin loans above 5% of their free float.

One view is that the move hurts brokers and exchanges such as BSE, NSE, CDSL and Angel One. The opposite view is that their profit impact is small, and the real damage is to small and mid-sized shares, where borrowed money props up prices. Dealers note that broking shares fell on the news and then kept closing near their lows.

Reading between the lines, a cap would arrive just when small shares are already weak. Dealers say companies below ₹5,000 crore in market value are "practically dead" to trade, so less borrowed money would make that worse, while the big names that foreigners buy are not affected.
